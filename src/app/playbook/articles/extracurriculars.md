### Story: The Drummer's Deep Dive
Mauykh wasn't the valedictorian. His GPA was solid, but not outstanding.
What was outstanding was his passion for drumming, which consumed every spare moment. He wasn't merely a band member;
he lived and breathed rhythm. He painstakingly arranged complex percussion for the jazz ensemble, tutored younger drummers after school, and even taught himself advanced polyrhythms from obscure online tutorials, filling journals with intricate patterns.
When applying to music programs, he avoided a generic "Band Member" listing.
Instead, he crafted impactful descriptions: "Principal Percussionist, Jazz Ensemble (2 years); Arranged and transcribed 'Giant Steps' percussion for regional competition. Initiated and led peer-tutoring for 10+ middle school drummers, enhancing per-student mastery by 30%. Self-studied polyrhythmic theory, applying concepts to original compositions."
While his transcript showed a B in AP Calc, his application painted a vibrant picture of a dedicated, innovative musician who took profound initiative and positively influenced others.
And he asked his band leader for a letter of rec, who wrote shining words of praise on his commitment to his craft.
The admissions committee saw past individual grades to the depth of his commitment and his authentic, profound impact.

### Academics: Beyond Just the GPA
Colleges analyze your academic record like detectives, seeking more than just a raw number.

**What They Really Care About:**
* **GPA Trends:** An upward trajectory, where you started rough but significantly improved, showcases resilience and growth.
* **Course Rigor:** Challenging yourself with harder math, AP sciences, or dual enrollment courses demonstrates intellectual curiosity and a willingness to learn.
    However, succeed in those challenging classes; don't overcommit.
* **Extracurriculars:** How you spend your free time says a lot.
* **Your School's Context:** Admissions committees understand your high school's unique curriculum and opportunities.
    You are evaluated based on the resources **available to you**.
* **Genuine Interest:** Did you pursue that extra history class out of passion, or merely to boost your transcript?
    Your authentic enthusiasm for learning always shines through.

📝 **Action Item**: Write down 5 different activities you do right now, either as an activity in school (think club, sport, etc.), outside of school, or even just as a hobby.
Write down what you’ve done, why you enjoy it, and what you’ve learned (hint: this is very similar to what you’ll be doing for your activities list. Then, think about how you can take this activity to the next level.

### Standardized Tests: The Ever-Changing Landscape
The role of the SAT/ACT in admissions has undergone significant transformation.

**The Latest Scoop:**
* **Test-Optional Dominance:** Over 80% of colleges remain test-optional for Fall 2025, allowing you to choose whether to submit scores.
* **Strategic Submission:** Only send your scores if they fall within the **upper half** of the admitted students' range for your target school.
    Check the common data set to see what the average scores are for admitted students to a given college.
* **Score Benefits:** Stellar scores can certainly bolster an application, particularly if other elements of your profile are less robust.
* **No Scores ≠ Rejection:** At test-optional institutions, not submitting scores will not automatically disadvantage your application.
    (Note: Some highly selective schools like Yale and MIT have reinstated requirements, so always verify policies for your specific dream schools.)

**Your Strategic Play:**
* **Practice Diagnostic:** Take practice tests to accurately gauge your current performance level.
    Set a timer to get comfortable with the given time frame.
* **Smart Preparation:** Utilize free, high-quality resources like Khan Academy and College Board.
* **Consider Retakes:** If diagnostic results indicate potential for improvement, attempt another sitting.
* **Align with Comfort:** Focus on schools where your scores (or the decision not to submit them) make sense for you.

💡 **Bonus Tip**: You don’t have to pay for good SAT practice materials.
Most of the time if you pay for a coach or a class, you’re only paying for the structure of sitting down and actually doing the exam.
Instead, save money by using Google, Khan Academy, and YouTube to find free tips and tricks + actual exam questions.

💡 **Bonus Tip**: SAT has an online and in-person version nowadays, and which one you take is entirely up to preference.
We find that most students (ourself included) feel more confident with paper exams where you can write, annotate, and “interact” more with the actual test sheet.
The actual content and difficulty of the exams is the exact same.

### Extracurriculars That Actually Pop
Take a look at these two extracurricular descriptions:
“Won a local robotics competition”
“Secured 1st place at my city’s local robotics competition across 50+ other participants.”
Which one do you think sounds better? Chances are you said the second (if you said the first … most admissions readers would disagree!).
It boils down to two major things:
* **Strong Verbs**: “Secured” just sounds stronger than “won”, even though the sentences functionally say the same thing.
    Use chatGPT or google to find lists of strong verbs and use them in place of weaker ones.
* **Quantify Your Impact**: Winning a local competition could mean anything.
    In the second example, the student clearly states what goes into it so readers really understand how impressive it is.

Move beyond a mere checklist of club memberships. Quality, depth, and impact universally outweigh sheer quantity.

**The Winning Formula:**
* **Meaningful Activities:** Concentrate on 4-5 activities where you are genuinely deeply involved. Depth > Breadth.
* **Demonstrate Growth:** Showcase progression. Did you evolve from a participant to a leader?
    Did your responsibilities significantly increase over time? This narrative is highly valuable. Colleges love a good story!
* **Quantify Your Impact:** Articulate specific achievements. How many people did you impact?
    What amount of money did you raise or manage? What was the scale or scope of your project or initiative?
    (e.g., "Managed budget of $500," "Impacted 200+ students.")
* **Emphasize Leadership & Initiative:** Did you start something new? Take charge of a project? Solve a problem creatively?
    Highlight instances where you demonstrated leadership or proactive initiative.

**Hidden Gems (Don't Overlook These!):**
* **Family Responsibilities:** Are you a primary caregiver for a sibling or grandparent?
    Do you serve as a translator for family matters? These real-world responsibilities demonstrate immense maturity and commitment.
* **Side Projects/Hobbies:** Did you independently code an app, write a novel, create a popular podcast, or master an unconventional skill?
    These self-initiated ventures reveal passion and drive.
* **Community Involvement:** Formal club membership isn't the only way to contribute.
    Volunteering, assisting neighbors, or organizing local events are equally valuable demonstrations of civic engagement.
* **Part-time Jobs:** Employment showcases significant responsibility, time management skills, and valuable real-world experience.

💡 **Bonus Tip**: Read **lots** of examples of successful extracurricular descriptions.
Your achievements are often more impressive than you realize, but learning how to portray this can be equally as challenging as the activity itself.

### Letters of Recommendations That Really Resonate
In your entire application, the only point where you can have someone else advocate for you is in the letter of rec.
That’s why it’s **crucial** that you start developing a relationship with your teachers early on.

**Choosing the Right Teachers:**
* **Beyond Grades:** Select teachers who know you holistically, not just as a numerical grade.
    They should be able to speak to your character, curiosity, and classroom contributions.
    Do you lead any clubs? Consider the teacher who supervises the club if they frequently observe your leadership skills
    Play a sport? A coach can attest to your determination and drive
    Frequent a certain teacher’s office hours? They can spill more details about your personality and commitment to learn
    Don’t do any of these?? Now’s the time to start!
* **Recent Connections:** Junior year teachers are often ideal as they've witnessed your most recent academic and personal growth.
* **Growth Story:** Identify teachers who observed you overcome a specific challenge, achieve significant personal growth, or develop a particular skill.
* **Balance:** Aim for a complementary pair: perhaps one teacher who can attest to your academic brilliance in a core subject, and another who can speak to your unique character, leadership, or creativity.
    Some colleges require one letter from a STEM teacher and one from a non-STEM teacher.
    Even if they don’t, we **highly** recommend you get this variety regardless.

**Making Their Job Easy (and Your Letter Awesome):**
* **"Brag Sheet":** Provide them with a concise list of specific memories, projects, or achievements from their class.
    This "cheat sheet" serves as an invaluable reminder of your strengths and contributions.
* **Share Your Goals:** Inform them about the specific colleges you're applying to and your intended fields of study.
    This context helps them tailor their letter.
* **Give Plenty of Notice:** Request letters as early as possible. We’re talking as early as junior year summer.
    If you can ask them in person before the year ends, that’s perfect.
    If not, ask them as soon as your senior year begins.
* **Express Gratitude:** A genuine, handwritten thank-you note after they've submitted the letter goes a long way and is a professional courtesy.

**But wait … How do I ask them??**
Don’t overthink it. If it’s a teacher you know well, just bring it up in person and follow up over email with the details we mentioned above.
If for whatever reason you can’t ask in person, that’s totally fine. An email will do.
One of the S4S founders, Keyon, remembers being **so** nervous to ask his teachers for a letter of rec.
He would go to their office hours, sit there, and have the period end before he could muster up the courage.
Eventually he dragged two of his friends with him for moral support and finally asked his teacher, who replied enthusiastically.
”I would love to”, he said, “I’ve been waiting for you to ask.”
Moral of the story: build relationships early (genuine relationships, not purely for the letter of rec).
And don’t be afraid to ask.
